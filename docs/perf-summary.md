## How to read this

This container (and CI) renders through **SwiftShader — software GL**, so `render ms` is the
cost of one `Engine.render()` on a CPU rasteriser at 1920×1080. It says nothing about a
player's frame rate; use it only to compare checkpoints against each other and to spot a
regression between runs. The hardware-independent numbers are **draw calls** and
**triangles**, taken straight from three.js' `renderer.info.render`; those are what the
regression fence in `tests/07-gallery-manifest-perf.spec.js` asserts on.

`Engine.getPerf().fps` is deliberately *not* tabulated per checkpoint: it is a 90-frame
rolling average of the RAF loop, which at ~1 software-GL frame per second covers a minute of
wall clock and cannot separate two adjacent stops.

### Findings

1. **Character rigs are the biggest bucket.** 6 rig types account for
   **320 draw units**, because a single humanoid body is 40–60 separate meshes.
   Triangles stay modest (~4k per body), so hostiles are call-bound rather than fill-bound —
   which is why a firefight barely moves the triangle count while the calls climb.
2. **Door assemblies are the biggest *static* cost.** 40 door groups contribute
   **157 draw units** at the lobby, more than the whole architectural shell, and they
   are resident for the entire mission regardless of where the player is. Each door is 6–21
   meshes (leaf, frame, stops, lever, plate, glazing). This is the cheapest thing on the list
   to fix: nothing in an assembly moves relative to the rest except the leaf.
3. **The world shell is well behaved.** The `world` group is ~290 meshes for ~120k triangles:
   most of the geometry for a fifth of the calls. Whatever batching it does already works.
4. **Traversal has headroom.** At the lobby roughly half of the visible renderables actually
   intersect the frustum, yet three.js still walks all 992 of them every frame
   (364 more are already hidden). The floor the player is not standing on is the
   obvious candidate for a coarse visibility toggle.
5. **Materials and shaders are shared well.** ~200 unique materials back the whole scene and
   only 20 shader programs are compiled, so pipeline state changes are not the
   bottleneck; 162 textures is likewise unremarkable.
6. **Software-GL render time is fill-bound, not call-bound.** In the table above `render ms`
   does not track draw calls at all (`copy_mail` at 399 calls costs more than `spawn` at
   1466): SwiftShader spends its time shading fragments. Treat it purely as a coarse
   "did something explode" signal and judge scene complexity by calls and triangles.

### Recommendations (for the owners of those systems — not applied here)

* Merge each door assembly into a static frame mesh plus a moving leaf. Worth ~110
  draw units at the lobby on its own (40 doors × ~10 parts collapsing to × 2).
* Hide the level the player is not on. The room data already carries the level and
  `world.lineOfSight` confirms the floor slab is opaque, so toggling `visible` on the other
  floor's group cannot change what is on screen.
* Consider `InstancedMesh` for the repeated prop parts: `tools/scene-census.mjs` lists
  geometries used 13–26× (the same box/cylinder/capsule across props). `--json` names them.
* Triangles need no attention: the worst case measured is a fraction of the fence.
